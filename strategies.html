What is the Sharpe ratio?

The Sharpe ratio measures return earned per unit of risk taken: it divides a strategy's excess return (return above a small risk-free rate) by its volatility. A strategy with a higher raw return isn't necessarily better if it took proportionally more risk to get there; Sharpe is the number that accounts for that trade-off, which is why it's used as the primary comparison metric throughout this project rather than total return alone.

Formula
Sharpe = (Annualized Return − Risk-Free Rate) / Annualized Volatility where, from monthly data: Annualized Return = (product of (1 + monthly return))^(12 / months) − 1 Annualized Volatility = std(monthly returns) × √12

This project uses a flat 2% annual risk-free rate rather than period-specific Treasury bill yields. Bill yields averaged roughly 1% over 2021–2022 and roughly 5% over 2023–2024, so the flat rate understates Sharpe ratios in the first period and overstates them in the second. Comparisons within a period are affected far less than the levels.

Interpretation
Sharpe RatioRead
< 0Losing money relative to just holding the risk-free rate; the risk taken wasn't compensated at all.
0 – 1Below average. Return doesn't adequately compensate for the volatility taken on.
1 – 2Good. A reasonable, generally attractive risk-adjusted return.
> 2Excellent, and over a sustained multi-year track record, rare. Worth scrutinizing rather than taking at face value.
A known limitation

Sharpe treats upside and downside volatility identically: a strategy with big positive monthly swings gets penalized the same as one with big negative swings, even though most investors are concerned mainly with the downside. The Sortino ratio addresses this by penalizing only downside deviation, and it is available as a chart option on the Strategies tab. Sharpe remains the baseline metric here, but it should be read alongside Sortino, drawdown and other risk measures.

Is this significant, or just noise?

Every Sharpe ratio on this site is a point estimate from 24 or 48 monthly returns, too few to distinguish a repeatable edge from the particular months that happened to occur. This table shows a paired bootstrap test (5,000 resamples per row, 90% confidence interval) on the gap between each active strategy and the benchmark: if the confidence interval crosses zero, the gap isn't statistically distinguishable from chance on this sample size.

What survives after trading costs?

Results elsewhere on this site are gross of trading costs. This table applies a flat 10 bps one-way transaction cost to each strategy's actual monthly turnover (the share of the portfolio bought and sold that month) and compares gross and net results.
