Do momentum and value predict stock returns?

A pre-registered test on about 500 S&P 500 stocks from 2012 to 2026. Do simple momentum and value signals predict next-month returns, and do portfolios built from them beat an equal-weight benchmark? The evidence is a null result, shown here with its confidence intervals and the limits of free, survivor-biased data.

Bottom line None of the three signals showed a statistically detectable relationship with next-month returns in the primary tests. The two portfolio intervals that exclude zero are the same finding measured twice (Value, 2012 to 2020, top decile and top quintile), and about two of 18 would be expected by chance. Because the first test period has 108 months, the test could only detect a mean rank correlation of about 0.055 or more, so the result means “not enough evidence”, not “no effect”. The data are free and survivor-biased: differences between portfolios are worth reading, return levels are not.

2 · Portfolios do top-ranked stocks beat the benchmark?

Growth of $1

Equal-weight holdings of the highest-ranked names, rebalanced monthly. The benchmark is the equal-weight average of every name that had usable data for that signal, so the comparison isolates the signal from the universe. The two test periods are chained for display only and are reported separately in the table. Levels are inflated by survivorship; differences are the informative part.

Sharpe ratios use the Treasury bill rate. Turnover is the average share of the portfolio traded each month, including drift. After costs means 10 bps charged on each one-way trade. A difference counts as significant only when the 90% paired bootstrap interval excludes zero (red).

Sharpe difference against the benchmark all 18 headline portfolio comparisons, before costs

Each bar is the paired bootstrap interval for the difference in Sharpe ratio between a portfolio and its benchmark (5,000 resamples). Eighteen intervals at the 90% level mean about 1.8 would exclude zero by chance alone. Two do: Value in 2012 to 2020, where the portfolio had a lower Sharpe ratio than the benchmark. They are one finding, because the decile and quintile portfolios overlap, and it does not appear in the re-test or the full period. Some secondary signal tests lean the same way, but they use the same months and stocks, so they are not independent evidence.

Membership is rebuilt month by month from Wikipedia. A name is usable when it has the price history (and, for Value, the book value) the signal needs. Companies that were acquired or went bankrupt mostly have no free price history, so the gap is largest in the early years.

Survivorship names later removed from the index vs names that stayed

PeriodRemoved namesStayed namesRemoved returnStayed return

Names per month and annualised mean monthly return. Descriptive only, not a correction. Names later removed from the index that still had a price history earned a lower average return than names that stayed, so the delisted names missing from the data plausibly did worse still. The size of that effect cannot be measured without delisting data.
